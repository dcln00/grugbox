Every November, the Ghanaian music scene goes through the same ritual. The Recording Academy publishes its Grammy nominations, local social media runs an audit of who submitted what, and the timeline splits into arguments over why Accra's biggest hitmakers were left off the ballot.

When the 69th Grammy nominations arrived on November 16, 2026, the list looked familiar at the top. Nigerian pop acts took up four of the five slots in Best Global Music Album, while British-Ghanaian singer RAYE led the entire ballot with 11 nominations. But on the ground in Ghana, the conversation centered on one specific track: "No Tradition, No Africa," credited to King Ayisoba featuring Rocky Dawuni.

It is the only song from Ghana nominated at the 2027 Grammys, competing in the Best African Music Performance category against records by Asake, Wizkid, Tems, Ayra Starr, and Angélique Kidjo.

### The record that slipped past the pop machine

"No Tradition, No Africa" does not sound like anything designed for club rotations or short-form video trends. It is built around the kologo, a traditional two-stringed lute carved from a calabash and covered in animal hide, played by the Frafra people in the Upper East Region. Ayisoba sings in a rasp that sounds closer to an incantation than a polished vocal take.

Panji Anoff produced the record at his Pidgen Music studio in Accra, releasing it in May 2026 as part of Ayisoba's seventh studio album, *Pure Confusion*. Anoff has spent over thirty years arguing that Ghanaian artists fail internationally when they copy trends from London, Atlanta, or Lagos. His production here gives Ayisoba's two-stringed riffs an uptempo pulse while keeping the acoustic dirt on the instrument intact.

Rocky Dawuni supplies the second half of the equation. Dawuni, who now holds five Grammy nominations, brings a measured reggae delivery that balances Ayisoba's raw delivery. 

### What the ballot tells Accra

The track's lone presence on the Grammy ballot carries an irony that hasn't escaped local listeners. For the past three years, Ghanaian industry panels and Twitter spaces have debated formula. Managers urged artists to chase Nigerian streaming numbers, align with major label subsidiaries in South Africa, or smooth out highlife melodies into mid-tempo afrobeats.

None of those commercial bets yielded a nomination this cycle. Instead, the Recording Academy's voters picked a record about cultural survival from a musician who got his start herding cattle and playing at traditional funerals in Bongo.

The lyric of "No Tradition, No Africa" warns against throwing away ancestral practices in exchange for borrowed habits. In an industry that often mistakes imitation for international ambition, the song ended up proving its own title.
