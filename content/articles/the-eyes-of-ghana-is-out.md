*The Eyes of Ghana*, the 90-minute documentary directed by Ben Proudfoot, is now available to stream free on YouTube. The release bypasses subscription services entirely, arriving directly on creator Darren "IShowSpeed" Watkins Jr.'s channel under an agreement between Watkins, Proudfoot, and Barack and Michelle Obama's production banner, Higher Ground.

The film follows Reverend Dr. Chris Tsui Hesse, who is now 93 years old. Through the late 1950s and 1960s, Hesse worked as the personal cinematographer to Dr. Kwame Nkrumah, Ghana's first president. Hesse traveled alongside Nkrumah across Ghana and abroad, documenting state visits, rallies, and diplomatic conferences. His camera recorded visits to Accra by Malcolm X, Che Guevara, Queen Elizabeth II, and Martin Luther King Jr.

### The 1966 coup and the hidden reels

When soldiers overthrew Nkrumah in a military coup in February 1966, the new regime ordered state workers to purge materials tied to the deposed government. Troops arrived at the Ghana Film Industry Corporation with instructions to burn film cans and paper records.

Hesse chose to smuggle the archives out instead. Over several weeks, he packed more than 1,300 film reels, representing roughly 200 hours of footage, into containers and hid them in underground basements and private storage spaces around Accra. His decision preserved what remains the most comprehensive visual record of Ghana's independence period.

### Preserving the archive

Hesse has lost most of his eyesight in recent years, but he spent the last decade working to save the deteriorating film stocks. The documentary pairs him with Ghanaian preservationist and filmmaker Anita Afonu, who runs the Heritage Film Foundation and has worked since the late 2000s to locate, repair, and screen historical Ghanaian celluloid.

The film was produced by Breakwater Studios, Anita Afonu, Nana Adwoa Frimpong, and Moses Bwayo, alongside Watkins. Watkins joined the project following his visit to Ghana earlier in 2026, where local fans introduced him to the preservation efforts. After discussing the project during a public conversation with Barack Obama, the team decided to upload the entire 90-minute film to Watkins's YouTube channel so anyone can watch it without a ticket or paywall.
