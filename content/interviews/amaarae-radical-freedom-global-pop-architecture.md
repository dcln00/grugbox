In the sprawling pantheon of contemporary West African musicianship, few figures have shattered conventions as decisively as Ama Serwah Genfi, known globally as **Amaarae**. Blending the sensuality of R&B, the defiance of punk, and the syncopated heart of Ghanaian Highlife, she has forged a distinctive sonic cosmology.

Following her celebrated festival appearances and the global reverberations of her groundbreaking albums, Grugbox caught up with the visionary singer, songwriter, and creative director in Accra. Over chilled palm wine and vinyl cuts, Amaarae opened up about her uncompromising creative architecture, refusing sonic categorization, and why radical freedom remains her only non-negotiable compass.

---

### On Carving Her Own Sonic Universe

**Grugbox: Your sound refuses to bow to conventional genre taxonomy. When people try to box your work into neat commercial categories, how do you respond?**

**Amaarae:** Honestly, I stopped caring about industry labels the minute I realized they exist to comfort executives, not artists. When I’m in the studio with producers like KZ or Kyu Steed, we aren't asking, *"Is this Afrobeats? Is this alt-pop?"* We are chasing friction. We want the kick drum to carry the pulse of an Accra street corner while the bassline evokes early 2000s London garage or Atlanta trap. If it makes my pulse quicken, that's the only metric that matters.

**Grugbox: Your live performances have evolved into visceral, theatrical experiences. What shifted for you between your early intimate club sets and commanding major international festival stages?**

**Amaarae:** Scale forces intentionality. When you have twenty thousand people in front of you, subtlety has to be weaponized. I look at artists like Prince, Grace Jones, and Fela Kuti—they didn't just sing songs; they commanded an ecosystem. Every costume, every lighting cue, every drum breakdown is an extension of the storytelling. You can't just be a singer anymore; you have to be an architect.

---

### On Accra as a Creative Anchor

**Grugbox: Even as your career takes you between London, Los Angeles, and Paris, Accra remains your creative anchor. How does the city continue to nourish your work?**

**Amaarae:** There’s an electric, chaotic rhythm to Accra that you cannot simulate anywhere else on earth. You hear it in the horns of the tro-tros, the street vendors chanting at dawn in Osu, the impromptu Highlife jam sessions spilling out of corner drinking spots. That energy keeps you honest. In Western cities, music can become dangerously clinical and algorithmic. In Ghana, music is still physical, communitarian, and deeply spiritual.

**Grugbox: What advice do you give to younger West African women stepping into production and creative direction, fields historically dominated by men?**

**Amaarae:** Learn the technical language. Do not let anyone mediate your vision. When you understand EQ frequencies, vocal chain routing, and arrangement structures, no one can tell you what is impossible in the room. Own your masters, protect your publishing, and never apologize for having high standards. If they call you difficult, it usually just means they couldn't exploit your brilliance.
