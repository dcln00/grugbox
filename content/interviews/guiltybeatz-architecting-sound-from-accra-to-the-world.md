Ronald Banful, the visionary producer and DJ known as **GuiltyBeatz**, has spent more than a decade defining and redefining the frequency of contemporary African popular music. From igniting the global dance phenomenon "Akwaaba" to scoring credits on Beyoncé's *The Lion King: The Gift* and *Renaissance*, Banful possesses a rare sonic ear that bridges African rhythms with European electronic music and futuristic house.

We sat down with GuiltyBeatz at his studio compound in East Legon to discuss his evolutionary production philosophies, dismantling Western misconceptions about African music, and why polyrhythmic precision is his greatest superpower.

---

### The Anatomy of the Groove

**Grugbox: Your drums possess a distinctive bounce that producers worldwide attempt to reverse-engineer. What is the fundamental secret to the GuiltyBeatz pocket?**

**GuiltyBeatz:** It comes down to human swing. So many producers quantize everything to a rigid grid in FL Studio or Ableton, and immediately the soul evaporates. Traditional Ghanaian percussion—whether it’s the gome drum, fontomfrom, or axatse—never sits dead on the beat. There’s a micro-delay, a natural drag or push that mirrors human walking or dancing. I program my percussion with my hands first, keeping those slight human imperfections. That swing is where the groove lives.

**Grugbox: Collaborating with artists across genres—from Mr Eazi and Tems to global titans like Beyoncé—how do you maintain your sonic identity while serving the artist's voice?**

**GuiltyBeatz:** You have to be a great listener before you touch a single knob. When I worked on tracks like "FIND YOUR WAY BACK" and "MOVE", the assignment wasn't to force an African trope onto an American pop track. It was about mutual respect and cultural exchange. You bring your heritage with confidence. When you know the history of Ghanaian Highlife, South African Gqom, and Nigerian Afrobeat, you understand how all these diaspora rhythms converse with Chicago house and Detroit techno.

---

### Cultivating the Next Generation

**Grugbox: African producers have historically struggled for proper publishing splits and backend royalty recognition. Has the landscape meaningfully improved?**

**GuiltyBeatz:** Things are getting better, but the fight is far from over. Ten years ago, producers in our part of the world were treated like beat-venders—you got a nominal upfront fee and zero master points. Today, young African producers are waking up to metadata, split sheets, and publishing administration. We are teaching the younger generation that a producer is a co-author of the record. Without the beat, there is no song to sing over.

**Grugbox: What excites you most about the future of West African electronic music?**

**GuiltyBeatz:** We are finally abandoning the pressure to cater to Western validation. Our artists are making 130 BPM club records, ambient soundscapes, drill fusions, and experimental electronics purely for ourselves. When you create without seeking outside permission, that’s when authentic magic happens.
